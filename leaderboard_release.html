FinTech News Impact Benchmark v1 A Latency-Aware Information-Rate Leaderboard for Single-News Triage Models

6551 News Research† In-house benchmark, public release
Version 1.0 n = 200 single-news items 12 models scored Released 26 April 2026

AbstractWe introduce a single-news, latency-aware benchmark for measuring how fast and how accurately a model can interpret a financial technology news item with respect to a target asset. The benchmark contains n = 200 timestamped news rows drawn from 7 catalyst categories, each annotated with a discrete direction label (long / short / neutral), a 0-100 impact score, and a one-sentence analyst rationale. Models are scored under a strict no-batching, no-cache protocol on an information-rate objective S=QPS·[−log2(1−A+ε)], where A is the mean of direction accuracy and macro-F1. The leaderboard combines twelve models spanning specialist news-triage systems, frontier general-purpose models, and small-parameter chat models. We report a mean-P99 latency split alongside the headline score and provide per-row commentary deriving each entry from its underlying components. The leading entry, newsliquid-1.0-flash, achieves 13.290 bits/s at 72.5% output accuracy and 140.1 ms mean latency; the highest-accuracy entry, kimi-k2.6, reaches 93.1% at 2 057.9 ms mean latency and ranks 8th by information rate. We publish the dataset, reference labels, scoring script, and full results.

§ 1Introduction

Public LLM leaderboards typically report a single accuracy figure on a held-out test set. For financial-news systems this measure is necessary but insufficient: an interpretation that arrives after the underlying market has already moved is rarely useful, regardless of how correct it is. We therefore propose an information-rate formulation that combines per-call quality and throughput into a single bits-per-second figure, scored under a strict single-news, no-batching, no-cache protocol.

The benchmark targets the operational regime of a real-time financial technology news pipeline: a stream of timestamped items, each with one nominally-affected target asset, that must be classified into a directional posture (long / short / neutral) and assigned an event-strength score. Scoring is intentionally indifferent to realised future returns; the reference labels record the analyst-grade interpretation a human reader would assign at t=0, not the realised K-line outcome.[1]

The remainder of this report describes the dataset (§2), the scoring methodology and protocol (§3, §4), the leaderboard with per-row analysis (§5), the reference-label schema with worked examples (§6), and the limitations governing legitimate use of the score (§7).

§ 2Dataset

The benchmark consists of 200 single-news rows. Each row pairs a timestamped news item with a designated target asset, plus a reference label set comprising direction, impact score, packed string label of the form [L|S|N][0-100], an analyst rationale (Chinese), and an annotator confidence figure. Items are partitioned into two buckets: important (n = 129), drawn to over-represent direct asset-level catalysts; and random (n = 71), sampled to retain coverage of weakly-related news, where the correct label is frequently neutral.

§ 3Scoring Methodology

Let l‾ denote the mean per-news end-to-end latency in milliseconds, a the empirical direction accuracy, and F1 the unweighted mean of the per-class F1 across the three direction labels. The benchmark reports four primary quantities:

QPS= 1000l‾ (1)
A= a+F12 (2)
S= QPS· [− log2 (1−A+ε) ] (3)

Equation (3) is the information rate, with units of bits per second of correct interpretation. The bracketed surprisal term measures the per-call information content — the number of bits of "non-error" each prediction carries — and the multiplicative QPS term linearly rewards throughput. The constant ε=0.0001 bounds the surprisal at −log2(ε)≈13.288 bits per call as A→1, preventing divergence and damping leaderboard sensitivity to the last fraction of accuracy.[3]

The choice to define A as the mean of direction accuracy and macro-F1 is deliberate. Because the dataset is long-skewed (96 / 60 / 44 across long / neutral / short), a constant-long baseline already attains 48.0 % direction accuracy. Macro-F1 averages per-class F1 across the three labels and therefore penalises models that achieve high direction accuracy by collapsing onto the dominant class.[4]

A third quantity, the mean absolute error of the impact score (MAE), is computed for audit purposes only and is not part of the public score. We retain it because impact-score quality is informative for downstream sizing and routing decisions, but treat it as orthogonal to the headline information rate.

3.1Latency reporting

We report both mean and 99th-percentile single-news latency. The mean drives QPS via Equation (1) and therefore enters the headline score directly. The P99 figure is reported separately and does not enter the score; it surfaces tail-prone serving stacks that a good median can otherwise hide. In production deployments where queue-blocking matters more than average throughput, the practitioner should weight the P99 column more heavily than the rank order suggests.

§ 4Evaluation Protocol

The protocol governs how a submission is generated and scored. Deviation from any of the five rules below invalidates the run for public-leaderboard purposes.

  1. One news per request. Items are processed individually; multi-item batches are not permitted.
  2. No response cache. Caching, deduplication of identical inputs, and reuse of previous outputs are disabled. Each call must traverse the full inference path.
  3. Labels withheld until predictions are written. The reference-label file (data/reference_labels.jsonl) is not loaded until all 200 predictions have been emitted, to prevent leakage.
  4. Wall-clock latency, recorded per item. End-to-end including network round-trip, provider router decisions, and any local serving overhead. Submissions report mean and P99 (with the underlying per-row latency vector available on request).
  5. Invalid outputs count as wrong. Predictions that fail to parse against the schema (or that emit an out-of-vocabulary direction) count as wrong predictions and still consume their full latency.

Submissions must additionally report the serving location, provider route, hardware class, and run date. These metadata are not used in scoring but are essential for reproducibility and for adjudicating cases where two submissions of the same model disagree on latency.[2]

§ 7Limitations and Honest Scope

This is not a pure model-quality ranking. Latency enters the score by design (Equation 3). Latency in turn is sensitive to network jitter, provider routing, hardware class, GPU load, runtime configuration, and the time-window of the run. Re-running the same model from a different region, on a different day, or under a different rate-limit allocation can shift the bits-per-second figure by a non-trivial amount.[2]

The dataset is short-horizon and financial-technology-specific. 200 single-news rows, weighted toward direct-asset catalysts (listings, ETFs, hacks, regulatory actions). The benchmark is not a general financial-reasoning benchmark and does not measure realised trading P&L or future K-line movement. The reference labels record the analyst-grade interpretation a human reader would assign at t=0, not the realised market outcome.

A fast specialist can outrank a more accurate frontier model on this score, by design. For low-urgency or high-stakes review, sort the table by raw accuracy or route uncertain items to a stronger model as a second pass. The score is built for the operational case where timeliness is itself part of the value function; it is not built for the case where the analyst can wait.[6]

Reference labels are human-annotated benchmark test-set labels. They are the official scoring reference for this public release and are documented in docs/ANNOTATION_GUIDE.md. The labels define an analyst-grade interpretation standard for immediate event impact, not realised future returns.

Public files intentionally avoid internal model and provider names. Rows in the leaderboard preserve the public model identifier as scored; the in-house specialist is marked with † in Table 4 and disclosed in note [7].

§ 8Notes and References

  1. Information-rate framing. The motivation for combining throughput and per-call quality on one axis is operational: a financial-news system that returns a correct interpretation 30 s after the underlying market has moved is rarely as useful as one that returns a mostly-correct interpretation in 200 ms. Equation (3) trades these against each other in units of bits per second of correct interpretation.
  2. Latency is not a pure model property. Mean and P99 figures are end-to-end wall-clock, including network round-trip, provider router decisions, and any local serving overhead. P99 is reported separately so that a fast median cannot mask a long tail. P99 / mean ratios above ~3× are usually evidence of router or queue effects rather than the model itself.
  3. The role of ε. The expression −log2(1−A+ε) uses ε=10−4 to keep the surprisal finite as A→1. The maximum attainable per-call surprisal is −log2(ε)≈13.288 bits.
  4. Why macro-F1 in A. The reference distribution is 99 long / 56 neutral / 45 short. A constant-long baseline therefore attains 49.5 % direction accuracy without doing useful work. Macro-F1 averages per-class F1 across all three labels, so a class-collapse strategy is penalised. Combining both into a single A term means the surprisal in Equation (3) cannot be inflated by simply guessing the dominant class.
  5. No batching and no cache. Submissions process one news item per request, with batching, response caching, and reuse of previous outputs disabled. Reference labels are loaded only after predictions are written. Invalid or unparsable outputs count as wrong predictions and consume their full latency.
  6. Second-pass routing. A practical production pattern is to use a fast specialist for first-pass triage and to route low-confidence or high-impact items to a slower frontier model (e.g. claude-opus-4.7, gpt-5.5, kimi-k2.6) for verification. The benchmark scores a single model end-to-end and does not reward such a composite system; production deployments should.
  7. Conflict of interest declaration. The top-ranked entry, newsliquid-1.0-flash, is an in-house specialist developed by the benchmark organiser and tuned for the operational regime the benchmark measures (low-latency single-news triage). Output accuracy of 72.5 % reflects an explicit speed-for-accuracy trade, not a quality ceiling. Readers should weigh this entry's score accordingly and consider sorting Table 4 by raw accuracy as a robustness check.
  8. Package contents. The full benchmark — input rows, reference labels, scoring script, and the leaderboard tables — is shipped under news_impact_benchmark_v1_public/. See the colophon for an enumerated file list.
  9. Run environment — unified Japan-region IP. All twelve submissions in Table 4 were issued from a single Japan-region IP via loopback routing to ensure environmental consistency across runs. For models whose primary serving region is not in East Asia (notably the rows flagged with the (i) network tooltip in Table 4: glm-5.1-fw, minimax-m2.7, qwen3.5-flash), the reported mean and P99 latency therefore include an additional cross-region network round-trip overhead and should not be interpreted as a pure measurement of model inference speed. The figures remain comparable as a deployment-realistic measurement under a fixed test environment, but a separate run from a region closer to each provider's primary serving location would likely move these latencies materially downward.
